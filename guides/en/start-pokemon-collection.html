1. Choose what to collect

This is the most important question, and it prevents most regretted purchases. There are over 40,000 Pokémon cards: nobody can have them all. Collectors usually pick a common thread:

  • A favourite Pokémon: every Pikachu, Charizard or Eevee card, from every era. Example: Eevee cards.
  • A complete set: every card in an expansion, from no. 1 to the last. A recent set or one from your childhood. See all sets.
  • An illustrator: some artists have an instantly recognisable style. See illustrators.
  • A language: Japanese, English, French… The same cards look and cost different depending on the language.
  • The most beautiful cards: full arts, holos, promos.

You can always change later. But a clear goal tells you what to buy, and above all what not to buy.

2. Set a budget

A booster costs a few euros, and rare cards are, by definition, rare. Opening boosters is fun, but it is a lottery: to get one specific card, buying the single is almost always cheaper.

A simple rule: decide on a monthly amount, keep part of it for the fun of opening boosters and the rest for the cards on your list.

3. Understanding the products

  • Booster pack: a sealed pack of random cards from one set.
  • Booster box: a box of packs from the same set (36 packs for standard boxes).
  • Elite Trainer Box (ETB): several packs with accessories (sleeves, dice, storage).
  • Collections and tins: a few packs with a promo card.
  • Singles: bought from a seller, knowing the condition and language.

For a focused collection, singles are your main tool. Sealed products are more for the fun of opening, or to keep sealed.

4. English, Japanese or another language?

There is no right answer, only tastes and budgets. Japanese cards are often praised for their print quality and come out first. Cards in your own language mean more to those who grew up with them. Prices vary a lot between languages for the same card.

Tip: stick to one or two languages to keep your collection consistent.

5. Where to buy without getting ripped off

  • In a shop: you see the card before paying and support a local store.
  • On collector marketplaces: Cardmarket, eBay, Vinted… Check the seller's feedback, ask for clear photos of the front and back, and compare with today's price.
  • Bulk lots: handy to fill a binder cheaply, rarely to find valuable cards. For example Pokémon card lots on eBay.

Beware of prices that look too good: fakes are common, especially holos and "gold" cards. See how to spot a fake card.

6. Protect your cards from day one

  • A soft sleeve for every card that matters.
  • A toploader for the best ones, or for shipping.
  • A binder with stitched pages, rather than a ring binder.
  • A dry place, out of the sun.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying every new product with no goal.
  • Opening boosters to hunt for one specific card.
  • Leaving cards unprotected in a box.
  • Paying for a card without knowing today's price, its language and condition.
  • Buying a "too good to be true" card from a seller with no feedback.

Frequently asked questions

Which set should I start with?

The one you like: the current set if you enjoy opening boosters, or a set from your childhood if nostalgia drives you. Recent sets are easier to find and cheaper to complete.

How much do I need to spend to start?

A few euros is enough: a booster, some sleeves and a small binder. The rest depends on your goal.

Do Pokémon cards go up in value?

Some do, many don't, and nobody can guarantee it. Collect for fun first. If value matters to you, follow prices over time and keep your cards in perfect condition.
